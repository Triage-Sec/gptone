**Abstract**

**The Impact of Sleep Deprivation on Decision-Making Performance: A Controlled Laboratory Study**

Sleep deprivation has become increasingly prevalent in modern society, yet its cognitive consequences remain incompletely understood. This study examined the effects of acute sleep deprivation on decision-making processes in healthy adults. Sixty participants (ages 18-35, M=24.3 years) were randomly assigned to either a sleep-deprived condition (awake for 24 hours) or a control condition (normal 8-hour sleep). Decision-making was assessed using the Iowa Gambling Task, probabilistic reasoning problems, and risk assessment scenarios. Participants also completed attention and working memory measures to control for general cognitive impairment.

Results demonstrated significant deterioration in decision-making quality among sleep-deprived participants. Compared to controls, the sleep-deprived group showed increased preference for high-risk, high-reward options (p<0.01), reduced consideration of long-term consequences (p<0.05), and impaired ability to adapt strategies based on feedback (p<0.01). These effects remained significant after controlling for attention and working memory performance, suggesting sleep deprivation specifically compromises decision-making processes beyond general cognitive decline.

These findings have important implications for occupational safety, healthcare, and policy decisions involving sleep-deprived individuals. Future research should investigate the neural mechanisms underlying these effects and potential interventions to mitigate decision-making impairments in sleep-deprived populations.

**Keywords:** sleep deprivation, decision-making, risk assessment, cognitive performance, Iowa Gambling Task
