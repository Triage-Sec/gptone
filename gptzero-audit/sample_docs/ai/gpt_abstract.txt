Sleep deprivation is a pervasive issue with significant implications for cognitive performance, particularly in decision-making processes. This study investigates the effects of sleep deprivation on decision-making abilities, emphasizing both rational and emotional components. Utilizing a within-subjects design, participants were subjected to two conditions: a well-rested state and a sleep-deprived state (24 hours without sleep). A series of decision-making tasks were employed, including risk-reward assessments and moral dilemmas, to evaluate changes in decision quality and speed. Behavioral data were complemented by physiological measurements, including cortisol levels and neural activity captured via functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).

Results indicate that sleep deprivation significantly impairs decision-making, leading to increased impulsivity and a tendency to favor immediate rewards over long-term benefits. Furthermore, neural analyses reveal altered activation in regions associated with risk evaluation and emotional regulation, suggesting that sleep deprivation disproportionately affects the balance between analytical and affective decision-making processes. This research underscores the critical role of sufficient sleep in maintaining optimal cognitive function, with implications for individuals in high-stakes environments where decision-making is crucial. Recommendations for policy and individual practice are discussed, advocating for strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of sleep deprivation on decision-making performance.
